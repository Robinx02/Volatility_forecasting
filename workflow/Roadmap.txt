PHASE 0: THEORY (Do this in parallel with data collection, Week 1)
You need to understand four bodies of theory before you can write the paper. Read in this order.
0.1 Realized Volatility and HAR-RV
Read: Corsi (2009) — "A Simple Approximate Long-Memory Model of Realized Volatility." This is the HAR-RV paper. It is short (20 pages). Understand why realized volatility has long memory (daily, weekly, monthly components), and why the HAR model captures this with a simple OLS regression. This is your hardest baseline to beat — if you don't understand why HAR-RV is strong, your paper's framing will be wrong.
0.2 GARCH Family
You don't need to read the original Engle (1982) and Bollerslev (1986) papers. Instead read the arch library documentation: https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/univariate/introduction.html. Understand: GARCH(1,1) is a weighted average of past squared returns and past variance. EGARCH allows asymmetric effects (negative shocks have larger impact). GJR-GARCH (also called TGARCH) adds a threshold term. Know the parameter interpretation for each. You are not deriving these — you are implementing and interpreting them.
0.3 Forecast Evaluation
Read: Diebold-Mariano (1995) — "Comparing Predictive Accuracy." This is the test you use to determine whether one model's forecast is statistically significantly better than another's. Understand the null hypothesis (equal predictive accuracy), the test statistic (standardized mean of the loss differential), and one-sided vs. two-sided versions. Also understand QLIKE loss: L(?², h) = ?²/h - log(?²/h) - 1. This is the preferred loss function for variance forecasts because it is robust to outliers in volatility.
0.4 TFT Architecture
You have the paper. Focus on Sections 4 and 7. Section 4 is the architecture (you already have the full breakdown from the previous response). Section 7 is the interpretability use cases — this is what you will replicate for INR/USD. The volatility dataset results in Appendix B.1 and Figure 4(d) are directly relevant — your results will look similar and you should compare explicitly.
0.5 SHAP
Read: Lundberg & Lee (2017) — "A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions." Understand TreeSHAP specifically (the efficient version for tree models). Know that SHAP values are additive feature attributions — each feature gets a value representing its marginal contribution to the prediction relative to the expected prediction. You need to understand this to interpret and write about your SHAP results. The shap library documentation has good examples: https://shap.readthedocs.io.

PHASE 2: DATA CLEANING AND FEATURE ENGINEERING (Week 1, Days 4–5 and Week 2, Days 1–2)
This is the most error-prone phase. Do it carefully.
Step 2.1: Establish the master calendar
Your master index is the set of days when the NSE was open. Use the Nifty 50 series as the calendar anchor — if Nifty has a return on a given day, that day is a valid trading day. Drop all other days. This automatically handles Indian banking holidays, which are not weekends and not in any standard Python calendar.
Step 2.2: Reindex all series to the master calendar
Every series (VIX, DXY, crude, etc.) gets reindexed to this calendar. US series (VIX, DXY, crude, US 10Y) will have NaN on Indian holidays that fall on US trading days — forward-fill these with the previous business day's value. This is standard practice and not a source of bias because you are lagging all features by one day anyway.
Step 2.3: Compute log returns
For USD/INR: log_return_t = log(USDINR_t / USDINR_{t-1}).
For Nifty, EEM, Brent, DXY: same formula applied to each price series.
Do not compute returns for yield series (India 10Y, US 10Y, call money) — use first differences instead: ?yield_t = yield_t - yield_{t-1}.
Step 2.4: Compute realized volatility (your target variable)
RV_t = rolling standard deviation of log_return over the past 22 trading days, computed at time t.
Specifically: RV_t = std(log_return_{t-21}, log_return_{t-20}, ..., log_return_t).
This means RV_t uses returns from day t-21 through day t. It is known at the close of day t.
When you build the feature matrix, your target for each row is RV_t (what you are predicting). Your features are all variables lagged by at least one day — so you use RV_{t-1}, not RV_t, as a feature. This is the no-look-ahead rule. Check this carefully.
Step 2.5: Compute HAR-RV components
RV_d_t = RV_{t-1} (daily lag, 1 day)
RV_w_t = mean(RV_{t-1}, RV_{t-2}, RV_{t-3}, RV_{t-4}, RV_{t-5}) (weekly lag, 5-day average)
RV_m_t = mean(RV_{t-1}, ..., RV_{t-22}) (monthly lag, 22-day average)
These three are both features for ML and the exact inputs to your HAR-RV baseline model.
Step 2.6: Build the complete feature matrix
All features are observed at t-1 or earlier. Target is RV_t. Every row is one trading day.
Features (19 total):
* rv_lag1: RV_{t-1}
* rv_5d: 5-day average of RV through t-1
* rv_22d: 22-day average of RV through t-1
* sq_return: (log_return_{t-1})²
* vix_level: VIX_{t-1}
* vix_chg: VIX_{t-1} - VIX_{t-2}
* brent_ret: log(Brent_{t-1}/Brent_{t-2})
* dxy_level: DXY_{t-1}
* dxy_ret: log(DXY_{t-1}/DXY_{t-2})
* india10y: India 10Y yield_{t-1}
* india10y_chg: India 10Y_{t-1} - India 10Y_{t-2}
* us10y: US 10Y_{t-1}
* us10y_chg: US 10Y_{t-1} - US 10Y_{t-2}
* rate_spread: (India 10Y - US 10Y)_{t-1}
* callmoney: call money rate_{t-1}
* nifty_ret: log(Nifty_{t-1}/Nifty_{t-2})
* em_ret: log(EEM_{t-1}/EEM_{t-2})
* is_mpc_day: 1 if tomorrow (day t) is an MPC meeting day, else 0 — this IS known in advance
* is_fomc_day: same for FOMC
Drop all rows with any NaN. The first valid row will be approximately 2005-02-15 (after the 22-day rolling window initializes). You should have approximately 4,800 usable rows.
Step 2.7: Train/OOS split
Training set: first 1,500 rows (approximately 2005-02-15 to 2011-02-01)
OOS set: remaining approximately 3,300 rows (approximately 2011-02-01 to 2024-12-31)
Save the split indices. Never touch the OOS set during model development.

PHASE 4: BASELINE MODELS (Week 2, Day 4 through Week 3)
You need four econometric baselines before touching any ML.
Model 1: GARCH(1,1)
What it is: ?²_t = ? + ?·?²_{t-1} + ?·?²_{t-1} where ?²_{t-1} is yesterday's squared return.
What data goes in: the full log return series (not RV — GARCH models returns directly).
How to run walk-forward: Fit on training set. For each OOS day t, generate a 1-step-ahead variance forecast. Then expand the training window by 1, refit every 252 days. Refitting every day is unnecessary and expensive. Every 252 days is the standard.
Output: array of OOS variance forecasts. Take square root to get volatility forecasts. Compare against your RV target.
Model 2: EGARCH(1,1)
Same structure as GARCH but the variance equation is in log form and includes an asymmetry term. This captures the leverage effect — negative return shocks increase volatility more than positive shocks of equal size. For INR/USD, this means depreciation episodes (negative returns for the rupee, i.e., positive returns in USD terms) create more volatility than appreciation episodes. Implement with the arch library, same walk-forward procedure.
Model 3: GJR-GARCH(1,1)
Adds a threshold indicator: ?²_t = ? + (? + ?·I_{t-1})·?²_{t-1} + ?·?²_{t-1} where I_{t-1}=1 when ?_{t-1}<0. Same walk-forward procedure.
Model 4: HAR-RV
What it is: RV_t = ? + ?_d·RV_d_t + ?_w·RV_w_t + ?_m·RV_m_t + ?_t
This is an OLS regression — not a neural network, not a tree. Just three regressors.
What data goes in: RV series only, using the three lag components you computed in Step 2.5. No exogenous variables. This is intentional — HAR-RV uses only past realized volatility.
How to run walk-forward: Fit OLS on training set. For each OOS day, generate forecast = ?? + ??_d·RV_d_t + ??_w·RV_w_t + ??_m·RV_m_t. Refit every 252 days with expanding window.
This is your most important baseline. It is harder to beat than GARCH because it directly uses realized volatility rather than squared returns.
For all four baselines: record OOS forecasts as arrays. Save them. You will compare everything against these.

PHASE 6: EVALUATION AND COMPARISON (Week 6)
Step 6.1: Point forecast metrics
For every model (GARCH, EGARCH, GJR-GARCH, HAR-RV, RF, XGBoost, LSTM, TFT), compute over the full OOS period:
RMSE = sqrt(mean((RV_forecast - RV_actual)²))
MAE = mean(|RV_forecast - RV_actual|)
QLIKE = mean(RV_actual/RV_forecast - log(RV_actual/RV_forecast) - 1)
Report all three. QLIKE is the primary metric — it is scale-free and standard in the realized volatility literature. RMSE is reported because readers expect it. MAE is reported as a robustness check.
Step 6.2: Diebold-Mariano test
For each pair of models (model_i vs. HAR-RV, model_i vs. GARCH(1,1)), run a Diebold-Mariano test to determine whether the forecast accuracy difference is statistically significant.
The test statistic is: DM = d? / sqrt(variance of d), where d_t = L(e_{1,t}) - L(e_{2,t}) is the difference in loss at each time step, and L is your chosen loss function (use QLIKE).
Under the null of equal predictive accuracy, DM ~ N(0,1) for large samples. Your OOS window (~3,300 obs) is large enough.
Use the dm_test function from the arch library, or implement it manually — it is 10 lines of code.
This produces a p-value. If p < 0.05, the better model is significantly better. If p > 0.05, the difference is not statistically distinguishable — this is an important null result to report honestly.
Step 6.3: Regime-split evaluation
Split your OOS period into:
* High volatility regime: OOS days where realized volatility > 75th percentile of the OOS RV distribution
* Low volatility regime: the remaining 75% of OOS days
Compute RMSE and QLIKE separately for each regime, for each model.
The research question this answers: does ML outperformance (if any) concentrate in high-volatility crises, or is it uniform?
Expected finding: ML likely outperforms more in high-volatility regimes. GARCH family models assume constant parameter structure — they adapt slowly to regime changes. XGBoost can capture non-linear threshold effects.
Step 6.4: Mincer-Zarnowitz regression
For each model, run: RV_actual = ? + ?·RV_forecast + ?
A well-calibrated forecast has ?=0 and ?=1. Test H0: ?=0, ?=1 jointly (F-test). This tells you whether your forecasts are unbiased. Report for your two best models at minimum.

7.2 TFT variable selection weights
TFT produces native variable importance from its variable selection networks — these are interpretable in a structurally different way from SHAP. SHAP is post-hoc. TFT variable selection is architectural — it is what the model actually uses during inference.
Compare TFT variable selection weights vs. XGBoost SHAP values. Do they agree on the ranking? If both say VIX is the most important exogenous variable, this is a robust finding. If they disagree, discuss why (SHAP and attention measure different things).
7.3 TFT temporal attention
Plot mean attention weight as a function of lag (0 to -62 days). Compare against what the paper found for their volatility dataset (roughly uniform across all lags).
If your INR/USD attention shows concentration at recent lags (e.g., lags 1–5 dominate): short-memory process, consistent with GARCH behavior.
If attention is roughly uniform: long-memory process, consistent with HAR-RV's motivation.
If attention shows periodic spikes: seasonality or calendar effects embedded in the data.
7.4 Regime detection
Plot dist(t) (Bhattacharya distance from mean attention pattern) against realized volatility. Annotate with crisis events. If dist(t) spikes during 2008, 2013, 2020 — TFT is behaviorally changing during crises. This is direct evidence that INR/USD volatility dynamics are regime-dependent, which justifies using ML over GARCH.
